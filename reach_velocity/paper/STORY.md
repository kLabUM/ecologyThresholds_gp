# The paper's story (revised after the reviewer pass, 22 September 2026)

Written for a hydroinformatics readership (Environmental Modelling and Software, or the methods half of Water Resources Research). Five figures, one message each (`FIGURES.md`). Every number below comes from `reach_velocity/data_clean/` and is traceable in the logbook; entry 22 records what the three reviewer passes changed.

## The claim, in one paragraph

Stage sensors now cost less than a day of field work and record every ten minutes, while velocity, the variable on which habitat and many operating decisions depend, is still measured by hand on a few days a year. We ask how far one stage record and one survey campaign can be pushed. The answer on a 0.7 km reach is: to the velocity of every surveyed cross-section at every hour of two seasons, with a band that holds up when whole sections and whole days are held out, and from there to an ecological number (the share of the reach below a velocity threshold) with a band of about eight points inside the surveyed stage range. The method rests on two physical reductions that make the spatial learning problem small: the log law turns each profile into one amplitude, and a one-parameter stage law turns the amplitude into a place factor that does not depend on the day (a separable index-velocity model, in the language of the USGS methods). A Gaussian process then carries the place factor across the reach on measured channel geometry, and a joint Monte Carlo chain carries the uncertainty. The two further reaches without a sensor show what the method needs: the spatial part reproduces where the water is not too slow, and the season-long part needs the local water level, which no gauge upstream or downstream supplied.

## The five things (one per figure)

1. **The target is a property of the place** (F1). The amplitude of a log profile, with one roughness scale for the reach (z0 = 11 mm, flat between 7 and 16 mm), is one number per location with a measurement error from the profile's own readings. Dividing it by (stage − 0.45 m) removes the day: the held-out day level has sd 0.34 in log and the law leaves 0.17. That reduction is what makes 257 locations enough.

2. **The place factor is at-a-station geometry, and its held-out error is known** (F2). A Gaussian process on six measured features predicts a held-out section with CRPS 0.31 against 0.41 for the reach mean (coverage 0.93), and beats the reach mean on 17 of 20 sections. Bank distance alone does as well (0.30, better on 15 of 20, p = 0.14): the field is mostly distance to the bank, with pool and riffle depth second. Random folds flatter every model by leaking neighbours (0.28 for the six-feature model), which is why the paper scores sections and days held out.

3. **One stage sensor carries the place factor through time, and the law is a ridge** (F3). Readings on held-out days are predicted to 0.082 m/s against a mean absolute deviation of 0.119 (below or above 0.2 m/s called right for 76% against 58%); at the section level, each day held out and the chain refitted without it, the measured section mean velocity lies inside the 90% band for 20 of 20 sections (MAE 0.048 m/s, median relative error 18%). The exponent of the law is not identified by eleven days (the day-out score is flat across (h0, γ) pairs from (0.35, 1.25) to (0.65, 0.5)); the measured rating implies γ ≈ 0.75 and the re-read ADCP 1.1 to 1.25, so the chain draws from the whole set and the band above the surveyed range says what that costs. The discharges through the 20 sections on 11 days, computed from the measured profiles alone, lie on one rating (Q = 28 (stage − 0.45)^1.70, 7% scatter), sections surveyed the same day agree to 12% or better, and at a common stage the modelled discharges of the 20 sections spread by 9% at 0.90 m and 14% at the edges of the range. The reach carries 77% of the USGS gauge 18 km downstream except on two days when a tributary storm reached the gauge first: the case for the local sensor in one panel.

4. **Spatial rating curves** (F4). The width-weighted mean velocity of every section every two hours through 2025 and 2026, with 90% bands of about ±21% at 0.90 m and ±29% at 1.30 m; inside the surveyed range the band is the field and the day-to-day discrepancy, above it the law set. The hours outside the surveyed range are marked (35% of the 2025 record, 50% of 2026).

5. **The ecological number, learned from the fish rather than assumed** (F5, and figures 10 to 12 in `figures/`). The convention was the share of the reach below 0.2 m/s. The nests replace it. Each nest is a used cell and 20 random wet cells at the same stage are what was available; a logistic selection model says what the fish chose. Bank distance is the strong selector (partly where the crews searched); velocity only shows once depth is in the model, because near the bank deeper cells are faster and nests take the deep-but-slow ones. With the USFWS spawning suitability curve (Edwards et al. 1983) as a weak prior, the velocity effect is −4.6 ± 1.5 per m/s (the data alone −5.6 ± 1.8), half use at 0.15 m/s [0.10, 0.32]. The ecological number becomes the reach's mean relative suitability E, no threshold at all: 0.33 [0.21, 0.57] at 0.90 m, medians 0.40 over 2025 and 0.34 over 2026, with the probability of exceeding any level at every hour, and with the curve's uncertainty and the velocity chain's combined. The share below 0.2 m/s stays as the plain-language companion (median 41% over 2025, 34% over 2026, band ±7.6 points inside the surveyed range); 0.2 m/s is the 61st percentile of the nests, so the convention was not far off, but now it is estimated. Two caveats travel with the number: the 59 nests on nine days give wider intervals when clustered by day, and the nests' own readings show the shelter is finer than 2 m, so this is a section-scale suitability.

## What the other reaches say (a table and a paragraph, not a figure)

The same protocol on the Upper and Lower reaches, which have no sensor. The spatial field reproduces in the Lower (CRPS 0.37 against 0.54 for the reach mean; 85% of held-out points called right against 64%) and does not in the Upper (0.43 against 0.38), a slow reach (two thirds of the points below the threshold, a 1 m drop over 7.7 km, amplitude 0.045 m/s against a reading noise of 0.011) where two adjacent 100 m stretches surveyed a day apart at the same water level differ by 0.36 in log a. No season series is offered for either, because the RTK water surface on the survey days shows that the nearest gauge is not the local water level: at the Lower, the surface at a section surveyed twice rose 62 cm while the gauge 42 km upstream rose 22% (a storm, a release from the dam the reach sits below, or lake set-up; the schedule is asked for). The Lower shows the field, not the law (its day levels relate to the local level at 0.3 per m against the Middle's 3.2). The design lesson is the sensor, and a second one is to interleave sections across days and repeat a few: the Middle's survey confounds day with place too, and its stage law is believed because the continuity of the measured discharges is place-free.

## What a reader can reuse

(i) The data requirement for a season-long velocity record on surveyed sections: one stage sensor in the reach, one campaign of three-to-five-point profiles on sections spanning the reach, RTK on the sections, and either repeated sections across days or an instrument whose reading is understood, to fix the stage law's exponent above the surveyed range. (ii) The reduction: log-law amplitude, then the one-parameter law, then a spatial model on geometry (bank distance carries most of it). (iii) The validation protocol: sections out, days out, readings on held-out days in physical units, sections on held-out days against the band, and continuity of discharge between sections at common stage and against the gauge, scored with MAE, CRPS and coverage rather than R². (iv) The joint uncertainty chain with its calibration on held-out days, the split of the discrepancy into a reach-level and a point-level part, and its attribution by source. (v) The survey design lessons: interleave sections across days, repeat a few, record heights above the bed consistently, grade every RTK session by the water surface's flatness, and know what the fixed instrument measures before deploying it.

## Honest limits, to be stated

The wetted width cannot grow beyond the surveyed points, so the share of slow water is a lower bound at every stage and more so above the surveyed range. The law's exponent above 1.09 m is a ridge that three weak constraints bracket, and the band says so; a high-stage survey or a revisited section at high stage would close it. The ADCP's level check rests on reading Vb as a single beam, which the instrument's configuration must confirm. The ecological number is a section-scale quantity at a stated reference height and threshold, and the nests do not validate it beyond the margin. Day and place are confounded in the survey design; the continuity of the measured discharges is the place-free evidence for the day effect. The transect labels, the height column and the row types in the survey sheets had to be corrected before any of this held (logbook entries 1 to 6), and the field protocol of one reading at 0.4 of the depth cannot feed the reduction.

## Abstract, draft 4 (for the hydroinformatics reader)

Velocity sets where fish can hold, spawn and feed, yet it is still measured by hand on a few days a year, while stage sensors now cost less than a day of field work and record every ten minutes. We ask how far one stage record and one survey campaign can be pushed: can they give the velocity of every surveyed cross-section at every hour of a season, with an uncertainty that holds up when tested? We reduce each hand-held profile to the amplitude of a logarithmic law, remove the day's stage with a one-parameter law, and treat what remains, a place factor, as a property of the location that a Gaussian process carries across the reach on measured channel geometry. Uncertainty is propagated jointly over the reach through the field, the bed, the stage, a set of equivalent laws and a discrepancy term calibrated on held-out survey days. On a 0.7 km reach of the Huron River, Michigan (20 sections, 257 locations, 11 days over two seasons), sections held out in turn are predicted with a continuous ranked probability score of 0.31 against 0.41 for the reach mean, readings on held-out days to 0.08 m/s against a deviation of 0.12 m/s, the section mean velocities on held-out days fall inside the 90% band for 20 of 20 sections, and the discharges through the 20 sections lie on one rating with 7% scatter. The result is a rating curve for every section and, from them, the share of the reach below an ecological velocity threshold every two hours for two seasons with a band of about eight points inside the surveyed stage range and a stated extrapolation above it. Two further reaches without a sensor show what the method needs: the spatial field reproduces where the water is not too slow, but no gauge supplies the local water level, so the sensor is the requirement. The reduction, the validation protocol and the survey design lessons carry over to any reach with a stage sensor.
